# The Producer: making the money

Read this when the owner's constraint looks like supply (work is won but stuck, late, too costly to deliver, or capped by a few key people), or when you need to size what realising more of their won work is worth.

*Source: Chapter 7 of* Forget AI. Show Me The Money. *by Aamir Qutub. Feeds the Revenue realised and Earnings lines of the Money Map and Worksheet 07.*

---

## Core ideas

- **Won is not realised.** The rainmaker fills the order book. The producer decides how much of it becomes real, delivered, invoiced money, and how fast. Work won but not delivered is revenue already paid to win, sitting on the floor.
- **The producer is whoever is in the flow of making and delivering the value,** whatever the title: the operator, the accountant doing client work, the engineer, the site manager, the account manager, and sometimes the admin. Value can jam at any desk. In one business the factory waited whenever the only finance person who could move a purchase order into production planning was away.
- **A finished good in the warehouse is inventory, not value.** Value is the customer holding it on the day you promised.
- **The four moves.** The old operations line is faster, cheaper, better. AI lets you do them at once, and frees you to do a fourth:
  1. **Faster,** so the cash lands and the next order with it.
  2. **Cheaper,** so margin widens as you grow (cost of delivering each job, not admin headcount).
  3. **Above and beyond,** so customers get more than they paid for. This is the crown, and where freed room should go.
  4. **Right,** so you never pay to do the same job twice. This is the leak, big enough for its own chapter.
- **On-time is when you get paid.** In some businesses (trading, for one) the next order only comes once the last is delivered, so late delivery freezes cash rather than just delaying it.
- **The real prize is freed capacity.** Beyond the hours, freeing delivery removes "the quiet tax called next year": the product line, market, partnership or acquisition never chased because everyone was flat out delivering.
- **The four steps (where to start).** Biggest money first:
  1. **Find the bottleneck** with the double-output test.
  2. **Give them sight,** so the person running it ranks problems by money and fixes them.
  3. **Take the grind off:** firefighting, checking others' work, repeated feedback, typing, chasing information.
  4. **Move one constraint at a time.** Clear the one that lets the most through, measure what it put on the sheet, use that to fund the next.

## Questions to ask the owner

Ask one at a time, and get counts.

1. "If work doubled next month, who would you pull into the room? What would break first if their load doubled?" (The double-output test. A bottleneck only matters when you push against it.)
2. "Is there anyone the work waits on when they're away? What can only they do?"
3. "Over the last year, how much work did you turn away or lose because you were too slow or too full?"
4. "How much shipped or finished late, and how long after it could have did the cash land?"
5. "How many customers didn't come back, and do you know why?"
6. "What does it really cost you to deliver one typical job? Where do the hours go between the work itself and the job being done (write-ups, checking, re-keying, chasing)?"
7. "Can your key people see their operation live, or do they work from numbers a week old, or in someone's head?"
8. "If you freed your producers' time, would you take on more work, or is the market capped?" (If capped, cheaper is the move.)
9. "What could you give customers that they'd never expect, if your team had the room?"
10. "What's your contribution margin: what's left from each extra dollar of sales after materials, freight, subcontractors and commissions?"

## How to size it

- **Keep three buckets apart.** They are different money, so never bundle them into one number:
  - **New sales** you could have delivered (work turned away or lost to slowness or capacity).
  - **Timing:** work that shipped late, so cash landed later. Money they were always going to get. This is a cash effect, not earnings.
  - **Retention:** customers who didn't return, so future sales must be won again.
- **Contribution, not revenue.** Extra sales are not extra profit. Delivering more work costs more. Size new sales at contribution, then take off any genuinely new cost and the AI all-in cost.
- **The book's worked example (illustrative, on its running manufacturer):** $1,000,000 extra sales delivered, less $650,000 cost of delivering them, less $50,000 extra system and running cost, equals $300,000 extra earnings a year. At 4 times, about $1.2 million added to worth. A million in sales is not a million in earnings. Have the owner do their own version with their own margin.
- **Cheaper:** a lower cost per job times the number of jobs, and it scales with every job they'll ever do, unlike a one-off cut. Only count it as earnings if the cost really leaves (hours redeployed to billable work, overtime or contractors actually stopped).
- **Freed capacity is a chance, not a return.** If freed hours become more jobs, size them at contribution and only if demand is there. If they go into above and beyond, the gain shows up in retention, referrals and price, and later in the multiple. Call it directional unless counted.
- **Measure realised, not hours saved.** Show worst and best, assumptions stated.

## Examples from the book

Illustrations, not benchmarks. Add no figures the book doesn't give.

- **The steel trader (faster).** Growth made the work heavier. Checking where the ships were took 2 to 3 hours a session, 3 times a week, up from under an hour. Each container needed a packing list of up to 45 minutes, 20 or 30 to a big order. Double keying into two systems sent containers to the wrong place. Instead of hiring two more people, the tracking and paperwork were taken off the team. The same team moves far more orders, and containers go to the right place.
- **The marketing agency (faster).** 4 account managers over a team of about 20, all buried in chasing progress, compiling reports, writing updates and handling change requests. Reports, campaign updates and recaps now draft themselves from campaign data and call notes; the account manager reviews instead of writes. Same-day turnaround, more clients each without service thinning, and freed account managers start making rain too.
- **The inspection company (cheaper).** Inspectors spent a couple of hours on site, then 3 or 4 hours writing up. The write-up cost about $400 on every inspection. With an app, the inspector talks and photographs while walking the site; the report writes itself and takes 10 minutes to check. That is $400 off every job across 30 inspectors, and each inspector went from 2 jobs a day to 4.
- **The niche sports-equipment maker (when cost is the game).** Already selling most of what its capped market buys in a year, with nowhere to expand. For them, leaner was the point. The book stresses this is the exception.
- **The author's own mapping day (above and beyond).** The author's firm runs a full-day session mapping a client's money and processes. The conversation is recorded with consent, and an agent turns it into an end-to-end process map with pain points and fixes, plus extras the client never asked for: a register of systems and costs, which tools talk to each other, a data-readiness check, a baseline of hours and money, a risk register, and a map of who the business can't run without.
- **The sweep (above and beyond in any business).** An accountant's client gets a 5-minute monthly voice note on how the business is tracking. A trainer's attendees get a learning module built from their own session. A resort builds each family a holiday plan before arrival. A party venue gives customers a planner for their whole event before they book.

## Where AI does the work

Describe the job, never a product or supplier.

- **Tracking and status checking** that updates itself instead of a person looking it up.
- **Drafting delivery paperwork** (packing lists, reports, client updates, recaps) from data already in the business, for a human to review.
- **Entering data once** and carrying it across systems, removing re-keying errors.
- **Turning spoken notes and photos into finished reports** on site.
- **Giving producers sight:** a live, honest view of their operation, and pushing what they need to know to them the moment they need it.
- **Taking the grind off:** first-pass checking of others' work, repeat feedback, chasing information.
- **Generating the extras** (plans, modules, summaries, registers) that let the business deliver above and beyond at almost no extra cost.

## Traps to avoid

- **Treating won as realised.** Ask what's stuck between the order and the bank.
- **Hiring into the bottleneck** before taking the grind off the people already there.
- **Counting revenue as earnings.** Always size at contribution, less new cost and AI cost.
- **Bundling the three buckets** (new sales, timing, retention) into one big number. Timing is cash, not earnings.
- **Confusing cheaper with cutting admin heads.** That's the small, capped saving and it shrinks the capacity they want to grow. Cheaper means cost of sales per job.
- **Making cheaper the whole plan.** It's one lever of four unless the market is genuinely capped or cash is tight with soft demand.
- **The "coolest kit" trap.** On a farm, talk jumps to sensors and cameras because they sound impressive. The question is what would move the number. Sometimes the answer is a system or a clearer view, and sometimes it isn't AI at all.
- **Trying to fix everything.** No business runs at 100%. Move one constraint at a time, biggest money first. You never remove the constraint; you move it.
- **Letting freed time evaporate.** Name where it goes.
- **Inventing a margin.** If unknown, work it out with them or mark it unknown.

## Where it lands on the Money Map

- **Lines:** the producer lights **Revenue realised** (the second line down) and, through cost to deliver, **Earnings**. Timing gains belong under **Cash**, never added to earnings or worth. Rework and redo belong to **the leak** (deliver right).
- **Worksheet 07: What is your unrealised work worth?** (a year): extra work you could deliver with the people and plant you already have; your contribution margin on it; contribution on the extra work; less any genuinely new cost to deliver it; less the AI all-in; equals real extra earnings a year.
- **The owner's next step:** find the work they already win but never fully deliver, and size what realising it would add, after the cost of delivering it.
