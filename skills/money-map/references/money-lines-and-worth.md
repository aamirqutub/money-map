# The money lines and what the business is worth

Read this when you are working out the owner's earnings, their worth today, or explaining why a steadier, less owner-dependent business is worth more on the same profit.

*From Chapter 5 of* Forget AI. Show Me The Money. *by Aamir Qutub. Coaching notes, not the book text.*

---

## Core ideas

**Most owners know what they earn, not what it is worth.** They know turnover, their best month, what they take home. Almost nobody has been taught the number that says what all that earning is worth to someone else. Treat that as a gap in what they were shown, never as a failing.

**Worth = Earnings × Multiple.** The book's plain version of a valuation: *"A business is worth what it earns, times a number."*

- **Earnings** is a normal year's operating profit: what is left after the real costs of running the business, before interest, tax and any one-offs. Keep it honest and consistent. A normal year, not last year's headline. Not the owner's drawings. An accountant has longer words for it and may tidy the figure at sale time; for finding where the money sits, the plain number is the right one.
- **The multiple** is the number the market sets for a business like theirs. It is always an assumption, never a market rate you can look up and quote as fact.

**Why this method.** Advisers use several: one leans on future cash, one adds up what the business owns, one suits fast growers and runs off revenue. A real sale may blend them. The book uses earnings times a multiple throughout because it shows most clearly how the owner's own hands move the number. Say so if the owner has heard of other methods.

**The three questions underneath every method.** Whatever a buyer or bank uses, they are asking:

1. How much does it earn?
2. How sure is that earning?
3. How much of it survives without you?

How much, how sure, how transferable. Earnings answers the first. The multiple prices the second and third.

**Easy to govern, grow and predict.** The book's plain words for what lifts the multiple:

- **Easy to govern:** nobody is nervous about what might be buried in it.
- **Easy to grow:** more road ahead than behind.
- **Easy to predict:** next year isn't a guess.

Signature line: *"Every point on your multiple is the market paying you for those three things."* Each is something the owner can build.

**Two levers on the same business.** Lever 1 grows earnings, and worth follows. Lever 2 lifts the multiple, and worth jumps on the same profit. Most owners only know the first exists, because the multiple only comes up at a sale, when it is too late to act on it. That is growth compounding twice: once into earnings, then again through the multiple.

**Why it matters even if they never sell.** Worth is the closest thing to an honest health score for the whole business. The bank leans on it to lend, an investor weighs it, a good operator checks it before joining, and it decides whether the owner can take a month off. If the plan is to hand the business to family, it matters more: a business that only works because the owner is in it every day is a hard thing to pass on. Signature line: *"The day you sell is the one day your worth gets read aloud."*

## The Money Map lines

Top to bottom, every business runs down the same lines:

**Orders won → Revenue realised → minus Cost to deliver → = Margin → minus Overhead → = Earnings → × Multiple → = Worth**

- **Orders won** is filled by the rainmaker.
- **Revenue realised** is what is actually delivered, on time, and invoiced. The producer turns won work into it. Won is not realised.
- **Cost to deliver** is where the leak sits: rework, waste and corrections.
- **The multiplier** lifts the multiple.
- **Cash** is the timing that runs underneath the whole sheet. It is never added to earnings or worth.

## Questions to ask the owner

- "What did the business turn over in a normal year? Any one-offs, grants or unusual jobs in there we should take out?"
- "What does it cost you to deliver the work: materials, delivery wages, subcontractors?"
- "What are your overheads: rent, admin, management, software?"
- "Is your own wage in those numbers? Is it a fair market wage for the job you do?"
- "Are any delivery wages sitting in overhead, or overhead sitting in delivery?"
- "Do you have a feel for what businesses like yours sell for, as a multiple of profit? Has an adviser or broker ever mentioned a number?"
- "If you were away for a month, how much of the business would still run and still earn?" (transferable)
- "How much of next year's revenue can you already see: repeat clients, contracts, recurring work?" (predict)
- "If a buyer looked under the bonnet tomorrow, what would make them nervous?" (govern)
- "Where's the road ahead: new customers, new markets, more from the ones you have?" (grow)

## How to size it

1. **Earnings.** Revenue minus cost to deliver gives margin. Margin minus overhead gives earnings. Show the sums.
2. **Multiple.** Use the owner's own figure if they have one. If not, offer a cautious range for their industry and size, label it clearly as an assumption, and tell them to check it with an adviser who knows their sector.
3. **Worth today = earnings × multiple.** Then show it **three ways** (Worksheet 05): market unchanged, a higher multiple they could earn by being easier to govern, grow and predict, and a softer market.
4. **If earnings are zero or negative, worth on earnings is nil.** Never multiply a loss.
5. Keep the two levers separate. Earnings gains come from the rainmaker and producer. Multiple gains come from the multiplier. Apply the multiple once, at the end.

## Examples from the book

**The running manufacturer (a worked example; figures are illustrative).** The book follows one manufacturer from Chapter 5 to the end.

- Earnings $3.6 million a year. At about 4 times, carried through the book **as an assumption rather than a hard market rate**, it is worth about $14.4 million.
- Lever 1: grow earnings to $5.7 million. At the same 4 times, worth is close to $22.8 million.
- Lever 2: same $5.7 million, but the business is now steadier, less dependent on the owner, cleaner and safer to take on. A buyer, bank or partner might value it at 5 times instead of 4. Worth about $28.5 million on the very same profit. Not one extra dollar earned.

Always present these as the book's illustration, never as what the owner's business will do. The full end-to-end version is on the running calculation page.

**The author's own background.** He spent years on startup valuations, then learned how real businesses are valued from people who buy and sell them for a living. His point: everything a buyer would do to lift the number, the owner can do themselves and keep the upside.

## The book's honest notes

- **Worth isn't the cheque.** Earnings times a multiple is what the business is worth. What the owner personally walks away with depends on debt, tax and the terms of the deal. Treat worth as the health score of the business.
- **The multiple is not a promise.** It answers to the buyer, the sector and the mood of the market. A business can tidy itself up while its whole industry cools. A steadier, less owner-dependent business makes the case for a better number; it does not guarantee one.
- **The 4 times in the example is an assumption,** carried through the book for consistency, not a quoted market rate.
- **Other valuation methods exist.** This one is used because it shows the owner's levers most clearly.

## Traps to avoid

- **Using revenue, drawings or last year's headline as earnings.** Use a normal year's operating profit.
- **Quoting a multiple as fact.** Always label it as their assumption and show three cases.
- **Single-number worth.** Always show unchanged, higher and softer.
- **Multiplying a loss,** or multiplying twice.
- **Adding cash to worth.** Cash is timing, kept in its own band.
- **Presenting worth as sale proceeds.** Remind them it isn't the cheque.
- **Giving valuation advice.** This is a thinking tool on their own numbers. Point them to an adviser for the real figure.
