# The Rainmaker: bringing the money in

Read this when the owner's constraint looks like demand (not enough work coming in, or not enough of it signing), or when you need to find who actually brings the money in and where they are losing it.

*Source: Chapter 6 of* Forget AI. Show Me The Money. *by Aamir Qutub. Feeds the Orders won line of the Money Map and Worksheet 06.*

---

## Core ideas

- **The rainmaker is whoever the money walks in behind.** It is a function, not a title. It might be the salesperson, the partner or manager who owns the client in a professional firm, the marketing that creates demand in a shop or product business, or, in many small businesses, the owner. Find the real one before anything else.
- **Point AI at the rainmaker, not the customer.** The move is always the same: take off the rainmaker everything that isn't the human part, and hand the hours back so they do more of the human part. AI does the preparation, the paperwork and the follow-up. The human does the trust, the judgement and the close.
- **The job has three parts.**
  1. **Generate:** create the interest and bring the opportunities in.
  2. **Convert:** turn enough of those opportunities into money.
  3. **Keep selling:** sell more, and more often, to customers already won. The cheapest money to win is from a customer who already trusts you.
- **The problem usually sits in one part, and it is rarely the one the owner thinks.** Most owners reach for "more leads". Far more often the money is leaking at the close, or in the customers they never go back to.
- **Know who you are generating for.** Before building anything customer-facing, find out who buys and how each type behaves.
- **Free them, don't load them up.** "If the machine can make the list, the machine can do the follow-up."
- **When the rainmaker is the owner,** step in when you have to, and step out quicker than feels comfortable.

## Questions to ask the owner

Ask one at a time, and get counts rather than opinions.

1. "When money comes in, who is it walking in behind? Whatever their title." (Check whether the answer is the owner.)
2. "Roughly how many enquiries a month, how many get a quote or proposal, and how many sign?" This splits generate from convert.
3. "How does your close rate compare with others in your line of work?" If they don't know, note it as an unknown worth measuring.
4. "How long does a quote take to go out? What fills your rainmaker's day besides selling?"
5. "Who is actually buying from you? Is it one kind of customer or several, and do they behave differently (when they browse, whether they ring or fill in a form)?"
6. "What share of revenue comes from customers who have bought before? What do you offer them after the first job?"
7. "What happens after a sales conversation? Who writes it up, sends the follow-up, updates the system, and how long does that take?"
8. "Are you thinking about putting AI between you and the customer, such as a bot or replacing a salesperson?"
9. "If your rainmaker had an extra day a week, what would they do with it?"

If they say "we need more leads", treat it as a hypothesis. Check the conversion numbers before agreeing.

## How to size it

- **Measure the close, not the clock.** The rulers are win rate, close rate and revenue per rainmaker. "We saved the sales team 6 hours a week" measures the tool, not the money.
- **Revenue is the sign, not the profit.** Extra orders won are only worth what is left after the cost of winning and delivering them. Size the gain at **contribution**: extra revenue times the owner's contribution margin (revenue less the direct costs of delivering it), less any new cost to deliver it, less the AI all-in cost. Then earnings times their multiple for worth, shown three ways.
- **Quotes are not sales.** If the move speeds up quoting, count the extra quotes, then apply the owner's own close rate. Never present quotes sent as revenue won.
- **Show worst and best,** built from the owner's own counts (enquiries, quote rate, close rate, average job value, margin).
- **Conversion usually beats volume.** They've already paid for the leads they have.
- **The book's worked example (illustrative only):** a manufacturer turning over $30 million on a 12% margin, about $3.6 million earnings, worth around $14.4 million at 4 times earnings. Freeing the rainmaker moves revenue from $30 million toward $36 million. The shape carries over; the numbers won't. Earnings from that revenue depend on the cost of delivering it.

## Examples from the book

Illustrations, not benchmarks. Add no figures the book doesn't give.

- **The solar installer (convert).** Aiming to grow 60% and certain he needed more leads. His leads were fine; his conversion was well below where an installer like him should sit. More leads would have meant more people to lose at the close. Signature line: "More leads won't fix a business that can't close the ones it already has."
- **The travel agency (convert, the warning).** Sold personalised holidays almost entirely over the phone. Worried about comparison sites, the owner replaced the salespeople with AI against the author's advice. Cost came down, and so did sales. The people who ring instead of booking online ring because they want a trusted human; that trust is the moat. Later the same AI was pointed at the salesperson instead: customer history on screen, a tailored deal ready, follow-up drafted the moment the call ended. A voice bot for post-sale refunds was fine.
- **The quote engine (convert).** A custom manufacturer where quotes took hours, sometimes days, so the sales team sat building quotes instead of seeing customers. An engine now builds the quote from the request in minutes. Proposals went from 6 a week to 20, putting an extra $40,000 of quotes in front of customers every month. The book is explicit: that is quotes, not sales. But you cannot close work you were too slow to quote.
- **The knockdown-rebuild builder (generate, know your buyer).** The team wanted a website chatbot. Looking first showed two customers: young families upsizing, who browsed at night and on weekends, and downsizers, who mostly rang or filled in the form straight away. A chatbot suited the night browsers and would have got in the way of the downsizers. The chatbot was right for one customer and wrong for the other.
- **The boutique retailer (generate).** The rainmaker was a 3-person marketing team buried in keeping the content machine running. A content engine that pulls product and pictures into on-brand, scheduled posts cut that grind from three people to one. The other two moved to testing offers and creating demand.
- **The accounting firm (keep selling).** The partners and managers were the rainmakers but spent their days reviewing juniors' work. The firm feared AI would drag fees down. Instead of automating the cheapest desk, faster review freed the partner, then AI drafted about 80% of a quarterly advisory report from the client's own numbers, with the partner adding judgement. Clients moved from a once-a-year tax return to advice all year, and their fees roughly doubled, for genuinely more work. (That clients value the advice at many times the compliance work is their view, not a measured figure.)
- **Capture and follow-up (any part).** A small recorder captures each conversation, with the other person's consent, and the machine writes it up, drafts the follow-up, sets the task and updates the system. Once every conversation is captured, you can see what the best closer does differently and teach it to the rest.
- **The 50-names trap.** An automation emailed each salesperson a daily list of 50 people to follow up. One said he never opened it.
- **When the rainmaker is you.** The author was his own firm's rainmaker for years. He now steps back in only to sell the first of each new offering, then hands it over.

## Where AI does the work

Describe the job, never a product or supplier.

- **Preparing the rainmaker:** the customer's history, the right options and a tailored offer on screen before or during the conversation.
- **Building quotes and proposals** from the request, in minutes rather than days, for a human to check and send.
- **Capturing conversations** (with consent): writing them up, drafting the follow-up, setting tasks, updating the customer record.
- **Doing the follow-up itself,** rather than producing a list for a human to work through.
- **Producing marketing content** from existing product information and pictures, on brand and scheduled, so marketers can test offers and create demand.
- **Drafting most of a recurring advisory or account review** from the customer's own data, with the human adding judgement and the relationship.
- **Serving the customers who want self-service** (for example, a late-night browser) only after checking who the buyers are and how each behaves.

## Traps to avoid

- **Agreeing with "more leads" too fast.** Check conversion first.
- **Pointing AI at the customer instead of the rainmaker,** especially replacing the human whose trust is the reason customers come. Post-sale admin is fair game; the sale itself usually isn't.
- **Automating the cheapest desk first** to save wages, instead of freeing the person who brings the money in.
- **Loading the rainmaker up** with lists, alerts and reports. Test: does it let them sell more, or just give them more admin?
- **Counting quotes or leads as revenue,** or revenue as profit. Size at contribution.
- **Measuring hours saved.** Measure win rate, close rate, revenue per rainmaker.
- **Letting freed hours evaporate.** Name what the rainmaker will do with them, or the gain never reaches the bank.
- **Ignoring the owner as rainmaker.** If it's them, getting them out of the seat may be the bigger prize (the Multiplier).
- **Inventing a close rate or benchmark.** If unknown, make measuring it the first step.

## Where it lands on the Money Map

- **Line:** the rainmaker fills the top line, **Orders won**. What those orders earn after the cost of delivering them belongs to the producer; when the cash lands belongs to cash.
- **Worksheet 06: Your rainmaker, and where you lose them.** Capture: who the rainmaker is (whatever their title); where the money is lost (not enough coming in, or losing them at the close; the book's three parts let you add "not selling again to existing customers"); the one rainmaker move, stated as a change in the work, not a tool; and what it could add in a year, worst and best.
- **The owner's next step (the book's "Your move"):** name the rainmaker, name which of the three parts they're losing on, and fix that one first.
