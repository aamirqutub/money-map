# What it takes, and the close

Read this when the owner asks what they should budget for AI, when they are comparing options or quotes, when you are sizing the spend on their one move, or when the conversation is ending and they need one concrete thing to do this week.

---

## Core ideas

**There is no magic percentage.** Owners always ask what the number is. The book's straight answer: nobody has a credible figure for what a business should spend on AI as a share of revenue, wages or anything else. The right number depends on three things that belong to the owner alone:

- how hard they are trying to grow
- how much cash they can put in without hurting the business
- how fast they can get that cash back out

So stop asking what the percentage is. Ask what the owner is trying to move, and what it is worth. The book notes that among US small businesses that pay for AI tools at all, the median spend is only about US$30 a month (US data, paid tools only). That is the subscription layer, not what an implementation costs.

**Budget for the whole change, not the licence.** The licence is the cheap part. What costs money, and what makes it work, is:

- **People.** AI has to be built, pointed at the real problem, then run and watched by a real person, the way any employee needs a manager. Left unmanaged it drifts.
- **Fit.** A generic subscription makes the business bend around the tool. A real implementation fits how the business actually runs, and brings the people along. Sometimes that is an existing tool wired in well, sometimes an integration, sometimes something built. What you pay for is the fit and the change around it.

**Spend on intelligence, not arithmetic.** The book's favourite line, from one of the author's senior developers: "Two plus three is not an AI job. It's a calculator job." Much of what people want AI for needs only a rule, a formula or simple automation that costs almost nothing and never gets it wrong. AI earns its keep where the work needs judgement, language, prediction or making sense of mess.

**Fund the first step, let the wins fund the rest.** The self-funding staircase:

1. Work out the first year of real investment, sized against where the money is.
2. Before spending, work out when it should pay back, and write down the assumptions. That is a reasoned expectation, not a known result.
3. Decide up front how much goes in before a **review point**.
4. When it lands, fund the next phase from the **cash** the first one actually frees, after its costs and any working capital growth ties up. Profit on paper is not cash in hand.

The staircase only works with measurement. If the owner cannot say what the first phase put in the bank, they cannot fund the second from it.

**The review point is a test, not just a date.** At the review, check whether the leading signs are moving even when revenue is not (reach, engagement, early pipeline), and whether the written assumptions still hold. If they are climbing, a flat revenue line is patience paying in. If they are flat too, stop rather than spend more.

**Put a range on it, even when you can't.** Write three figures: worst, most likely, best. At minimum pin the two ends. Write hard-to-price moves as a hypothesis in plain money, then measure against it once live. The book's signature line: a range you can aim at and correct; a feeling you cannot.

**The close (Chapter 16).** Knowing where the money is does not move it. The book ends on one thing to do this week, not ten:

1. Name **one constraint** the owner already suspects is holding the money back. One honest candidate, not a perfect diagnosis.
2. **Check it with the people who do that work.**
3. Put a rough range on it (worst and best) and the cash it would take or free, then run **the three-question start test**.
4. Commit to **one action this week**.

## Questions to ask the owner

- How hard are you trying to grow? How much cash could you put in without hurting the business? How fast do you need it back?
- What is the one initiative you are closest to spending on?
- What could it move in a year, at worst and at best?
- What is the full running cost: the build, the person who runs it, the help, not just the licence?
- How much cash does it need along the way, and how much might it free?
- When do you expect it to pay back, and what assumptions does that rest on?
- How long will you give it before you judge it, and what leading signs will you check?
- Does this need intelligence, or is some of it a rule or formula?
- If you have several quotes, what does each net after its cost, at full success and if it only half works?
- (At the close) What is the one constraint you suspect? Who does that work, and what did they say? What is your one action this week?

## How to size it

Worksheet 15, "Size the spend":

- **Range it could move in a year:** worst $__ / best $__. Count new sales at margin, never revenue.
- **Full running cost:** the build and the person to run it, not the licence.
- **Cash it needs / frees:** kept separate, never added to earnings or worth.
- **Expected payback:** in months, with stated assumptions.
- **Review point:** how long you give it before you judge it.
- Then the **start test**: Is the likely result clearly worth more than the cost? Is the worst case one you could live with, including the cash it ties up? Does the first, smallest step test the thing you are least sure about? All three yes, start. Any no, it is the wrong initiative, or more homework on the prize.

**Comparing options.** Compare each on:
- what it is expected to net after its cost
- how much of that survives if it only half works
- how much cash it needs along the way
- whether the business can actually pull it off

Choose on what is left after the cost in the good case and the ordinary one, not on the sticker. Freed hours are priced at the value of the work the person moves to.

## Examples from the book

**The content and video engine (Chapter 15, real, anonymised).** The author's firm ran an automated content and video marketing engine alongside a client's existing agency, on the organic side. With no clean baseline, they wrote a hypothesis as a range: $200,000 to $250,000 a month in extra sales, for a cost of $15,000 a month. Almost nothing moved for the first 3 months; a review window had been agreed up front, so nobody killed it early. Extra revenue started showing in month 4 and settled at about $180,000 a month by month 9, below the range. Tracking to the channel was best effort, not audited. On a **representative 30% margin (illustrative)** that leaves about $54,000, and after the $15,000 cost roughly $39,000 a month of contribution at that margin, before counting brand, worth or compounding. The lesson: a range is a compass, not a scorecard. The result undershot the hypothesis and was still well ahead.

**Choosing on price (same decision).** One of 3 or 4 options cost $6,000 a month and was expected to bring about $30,000 a month in extra sales. On the same 30% margin, that is $9,000 gross at full success, about $3,000 a month over cost if everything went perfectly. One soft month and it is underwater. The dearer engine, even under its own range, cleared more than 12 times the profit the cheap option would have made at full success. Price is not the decision; headroom is.

**The author's Money Map rule (Chapter 16).** Nothing gets rolled out across a department or the company until it has been through a 10 minute Money Map exercise: which line does this move, by how much, and what does it cost? The proposal tool that passed it took turnaround from 3 days to 30 minutes and lifted closed work by around 40%.

## Traps to avoid

- **Asking for a benchmark percentage.** There isn't a credible one.
- **Budgeting for the licence only.** Budget for the engine and the manager at the wheel.
- **Paying intelligence to do arithmetic.**
- **Treating payback as known.** It is an expectation with stated assumptions and a review point.
- **Funding the next step from paper profit.** Use the cash actually freed.
- **No measurement.** Without it the staircase collapses.
- **Judging too early, or too late.** Set the review point before starting.
- **No number at all.** A rough range beats a feeling.
- **Choosing on the sticker.** Compare net, downside, cash and feasibility.
- **Ending without an action.** The close is one constraint, checked with the people, tested, and one action this week.

## Where it lands on the Money Map

- The range and realism feed **Worth** (worst, best, realism, counted).
- Running cost, cash and the cost ceiling fill **It takes**. Cash stays separate from earnings and worth.
- The start test fills **Start test**; the review point fills **Review**; the smallest step that tests the unknown fills **First step**.
- **Worksheets:** Worksheet 15 (size the spend) and Worksheet 16 (your first move: the constraint you suspect, what the people who do the work said, the three questions as Y/N, and your one action this week).
- Once that move is done, the next bottleneck gets a fresh map.
