# The three engines

Read this when the owner describes their business, their team or what is holding them back, and you need to work out which engine is pinching before you go anywhere near AI.

*From Chapter 4 of* Forget AI. Show Me The Money. *by Aamir Qutub. Coaching notes, not the book text.*

---

## Core ideas

**Every business is different on the surface and the same underneath.** How a business makes money varies a lot. The book groups the surface patterns into a handful of shapes, and most businesses are one of them or a blend:

- **Job by job:** win a project, deliver it, get paid, go again (builders, web developers, consultants).
- **In cycles:** win a client once, serve them on repeat (accountants, agencies on retainer, subscriptions).
- **Make and sell:** one loop makes the product and holds stock, another creates demand and moves it (manufacturers, product brands).
- **Buy and resell:** buy well, sell at a margin, over and over (retailers, wholesalers, distributors).
- **Broker the deal:** connect two sides and take a cut (real estate agents, brokers, recruiters).

These shapes are illustrative, not a complete list. Their only job in coaching is to help the owner see how their money moves. Underneath every one of them sit the same three engines.

**The three engines, in the book's own two-verb lines:**

1. **The Rainmaker** wins the work and brings the money in.
2. **The Producer** delivers the work and makes the money.
3. **The Multiplier** runs the business and builds what it's worth.

**An engine is a function, not a seat.** Do not look for these on the org chart. The rainmaker is rarely a tidy sales department. In the book's examples it was a manager or partner in accounting firms, a business development manager in a manufacturer, the marketer in a product business, and an operations lead who held every client relationship in his head in a logistics company. The producer is whoever turns the promise into the thing the customer paid for: production in a factory, junior accountants in a firm, inspectors in the field, engineers in a consultancy.

**One person can run two engines.** In a small business the owner often runs all three. An engine can also sit somewhere nobody thought to look, such as the long-serving staff member who started on the phones and became the backbone of the place.

**The owner is often the bottleneck, not the hero.** The book is blunt about this: more often than not, the person the business leans on most is the owner. When a business cannot run without its owner, it is worth far less to everyone except the owner. So the multiplier engine is rarely about doing more. It is about making the business need its heroes less, the owner most of all. The author admits he was the bottleneck in his own business, so this is said with sympathy, not blame.

**Two engines earn, one builds the worth.** The rainmaker and producer decide what the business earns. The multiplier decides what those earnings are worth, because it moves the number the earnings get multiplied by. The two are linked: grow earnings and worth rises with them, then the multiplier lifts it again. Signature line: *"Two engines grow what you earn. The third lifts what those earnings are worth."*

**Change the question.** The chapter's turning point is a critique of the standard consulting playbook (survey, readiness check, interviews, pain points, use cases, ranking matrix, build the top few). It works, but it hangs on pain, and pain is a feeling, not a number. Teams describe what bothers them, which is often not what moves the money. Signature line: *"Stop asking where it hurts. Start asking what would move the numbers."*

**The engines are old; AI changes how far and how fast you can move them.** The limits inside each engine always existed. AI lets an owner move them further and sooner, including some that were out of reach before. It is never framed as cheap or effortless.

## Questions to ask the owner

Ask one at a time, and prefer counts to opinions.

- "Walk me through one recent job, from the first hello to the last payment. Where does the money come from, and when does it land?"
- "Which of these shapes is closest to how you make money: job by job, in cycles, make and sell, buy and resell, or brokering? Or a blend?"
- "Who actually wins the work here? Not their title: who does the customer trust?"
- "Who turns a signed job into the thing the customer paid for?"
- "Who holds the place together? If they disappeared for a month, what would stop?"
- "How many of those three jobs run through you personally?"
- "If work doubled next month, who would you pull into the room, and what would break first?"
- "Which is the real limit right now: you can't get enough work in, you can't get enough good work out, you're short of cash, or too much rides on you?"
- "How much bigger do you want this business to be, and by when? A real number and a real date."

## How to size it

This page diagnoses; it does not size. Use it to choose where to start, then size the move on the engine pages and the running calculation. Two rules carry over:

- Rainmaker and producer gains land in **earnings**. Multiplier gains land in **the multiple**. Never count one gain in both places.
- The owner's goal (how much bigger, by when) is the yardstick for every move. Ask for it before sizing anything. The book says it can show the levers, but where to point them is the owner's call.

## Examples from the book

**Asif and the napkin at the tea stall (real, anonymised; name and details changed in the book).** Asif is the younger brother of the author's closest friend. He built a custom furniture business in India through sheer graft, then it stalled. He was working 14 to 16 hour days fighting fires and could not push it past a ceiling. On a visit home, the author sat with him at a roadside chai stall, pulled napkins off the counter, and asked him to walk through how the business actually made its money: where customers came from, how enquiries became paying jobs, how each piece was made and delivered, and when the money landed. He was following the money, not thinking about AI.

What they found was an honest mix: some habits that had crept in, a system he had never put in place, and some work for AI. The point the author keeps returning to is that AI was the enabler. Asif was so buried that he could never have found the room to make the other changes. AI took enough off his plate for him to lift his head and work on the business, not just in it. He broke through the ceiling. The author is clear that the credit is Asif's: he asked questions and scribbled on a napkin, and Asif did the hard part.

Use this story to show an owner that the method starts with the money path, not the technology, and that the owner-as-bottleneck is common and fixable. Do not quote any figures for Asif's business; the book gives none.

**The owners who pushed back.** When the author started mapping money before talking about AI, owners asked why he needed it when they had already listed their pain points, and one joked that he sounded like their accountant. Useful if an owner resists walking the money path: the book's answer is to stay with it, because following the money brings the real shape of the business to the surface.

## Traps to avoid

- **Mapping engines to job titles.** "Sales is our rainmaker" is often wrong. Ask who the customer actually trusts.
- **Treating pain points as evidence.** The loudest complaint is often not the money constraint. Test the owner's first answer as a hypothesis.
- **Letting the owner off the hook gently, or blaming them harshly.** Name the owner-as-bottleneck plainly, with the author's own admission as company.
- **Leading with AI.** The engines come first. AI is the means of moving a limit, not the starting point.
- **Assuming one engine per person.** In small businesses, one person often runs two or three.
- **Inventing numbers for the stories.** Asif's story has no figures. Keep it qualitative.
- **Recommending a tool or supplier.** Describe the job the AI would do, not the product.

## Where it lands on the Money Map / which worksheet

- **Rainmaker** fills the top line: orders won.
- **Producer** turns won work into revenue realised and protects margin (the leak sits inside cost to deliver).
- **Multiplier** lifts the multiple.
- **Cash** runs underneath as timing.

**Worksheet 04: Where does it pinch?** The owner ticks the real limit: demand (rainmaker), supply (producer and the leak), cash, or too much riding on them (multiplier).
