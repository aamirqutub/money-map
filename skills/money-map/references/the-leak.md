# The Leak

Read this when the owner mentions rework, call-backs, returns, corrections or warranty work, when the constraint looks like supply, or when they want to cut wages to save cost.

---

## Core ideas

- **The leak is money already earned, already paid for, then handed back.** The job done twice. The part sent back down the line. The wrong item shipped, returned and reshipped. The report that goes out wrong and comes back with an angry email. It is the fourth Producer move, "deliver right": delivering cheaper lowers what a job costs, delivering right means not paying for the same job twice.
- **People slip, most of all at volume.** A good process catches a lot, never everything. The team are not careless, they are human: tired, on the 40th one that day, with two suppliers whose names blur together.
- **It is small, constant and everywhere, but rarely even.** Most of the leak usually traces back to one person, one part or one step that keeps going wrong. Help the owner find that concentration.
- **It hides on the P&L.** There is no "rework" line. It sits a little in wages, materials, freight, and a customer who quietly never came back. What the owner cannot point to, they never cut.
- **It costs twice.** Once in money, and again in confidence. A process that leaks a different amount every week cannot promise a date or scale.
- **Cost your errors before your people.** Owners reach for the cost line they can see, wages. The book reverses that: before cutting a single wage, add up what mistakes cost and what fixing them costs. That is often the bigger number, and the one to cut first.
- **Point AI at checking, not making.** Signature line: "Everyone points AI at making things. The money is in pointing it at checking them." Generating content is the eye-catching use and the one that leaks fastest when unchecked. The use that pays is reviewing work before it leaves, at a volume no person could keep up with.

## Questions to ask the owner

Ask one at a time, and push for counts and dollars.

1. Over the last year, how often did work have to be redone, a job go back, an order get returned, or a document get corrected?
2. Take the most common one. What did it cost to put right: materials, labour hours, call-out or freight, and admin?
3. Did any of those mistakes cost you a customer or a referral? Roughly what is that customer worth a year?
4. Is it spread out, or does most of it come back to one person, product, supplier or step?
5. Where is the error made, where is it found, and how long is the gap?
6. Who checks the work before it goes out now? What share of it do they actually look at?
7. If work doubled next month, would the error rate hold, or would checking be the first thing to slip?
8. When there is a dispute, is there a record (photos, notes, documents) to go back to?

If they say their work is too complex or full of judgement to check, use the new-hire test (below) rather than arguing.

## How to size it

Build it bottom-up. Never accept a guessed total. This is Worksheet 08, "Your leak, bottom-up".

| Error type | Times a year | Cost to put right (materials + labour + call-out + customer lost) | Gross leak |
|---|---|---|---|
| ____ | ____ | $____ | $____ |
| ____ | ____ | $____ | $____ |
| **Total gross leak** | | | **$____** |

Then:

1. **Gross leak** = times a year × cost to put right, for each error type. Add them up.
2. **Claw back a slice, not the whole number.** Not all of it is avoidable and no checking system catches everything. Start at 30%. Cap it at 70%. Recoverable a year = gross leak × the slice.
3. **Show worst and best** (for example the 30% slice and the highest slice the owner can defend), then apply realism as the front page sets out.
4. **Count it with discipline.** Sort every dollar into one of three kinds:
   - **A bill you genuinely stop paying** (materials, freight, subcontractor call-outs) is money back today.
   - **Time freed from redoing work** goes through the three fates test. It is worth $0 until the cost is actually removed or the hours are pointed at something that earns.
   - **A customer you keep** is future business, not cash in hand. Note it, but do not bank it as this year's saving.
5. **Count each dollar once.** If a freed hour is already counted in a Producer move, do not count it again here.

**Industry reference, used carefully.** In construction, surveys of builders have put direct rework at around 5% of what a project costs to build, higher once indirect and latent costs are counted, and projects that track it closely often find far less. That is a share of project cost, not turnover, and it is the whole industry loss, not what AI recovers. Use it only to show a builder that the number is worth measuring. Never substitute it for their own count.

## Examples from the book

Describe these as the book's examples. Never present their outcomes as what this owner will get.

- **The bulk builder who photographed every job (the anchor).** A builder putting up homes in volume had small on-site misses turning into expensive call-backs months later. Every trade, on finishing, photographed the work and recorded a short voice note through a simple app. AI reviewed the photos and notes, judged done or missed, and flagged anything it was unsure of for a person, who could quickly mark the problem. People also spot-checked some of what it passed, testing what it missed, not just what it caught. Corrections fed back and it improved month by month. Honest limits: some things cannot be checked from a photo by anyone, some problems appear later, and it took about 9 months to pay its way. Worth it because at bulk even a small drop in rework saved a lot, and the record made later disputes shorter.
- **Trading and logistics.** One wrong document causes a very expensive problem. AI scans the emails and documents and checks that the proof of delivery arrived, the items are right, and the packing list, purchase order, supplier invoice and delivery all match. No one squinting at 4 screens.
- **Accounting and law firms.** The machine worth having reads the document, scores it against the firm's standard, and flags what is wrong before the client sees it.
- **Marketing teams and agencies.** Content checked against brand and facts before release. An agency's proposal and final delivery checked against meeting transcripts of what the client asked for, so it is right first time.
- **The factory floor.** A camera on the line catches the defect, counts the parts, and confirms the right product went in the right box before it ships.
- **The big-four report trap.** One of the big four consulting firms handed a government department a report built with AI and never properly checked. It quoted a court case that was never said and cited papers that did not exist, and part of the fee was paid back. Unchecked AI output is itself a leak, at any size.

## Where AI does the work

Describe the job, never a product.

- **Write the rubric first.** Teach the machine what good and bad look like against the owner's own standard. That standard is the real asset.
- **The new-hire test.** Could a sharp, qualified person sitting beside you for 2 or 3 months learn to review at least 80% of this work properly? If yes, it is worth testing whether a machine can, on your work and against your standard. The aim is not the last, hard 20% of judgement. It is the ordinary slips that happen because, at volume, nobody can look at everything.
- **Review, flag, decide.** AI reviews finished work (photos, voice notes, documents, camera feeds), matches documents to each other, and flags what looks wrong or uncertain. A person makes the call.
- **Test what it misses, not just what it catches.** Spot-check a share of what it passes, feed every correction back, and expect it to start rough and improve.
- **Keep the record.** The evidence the check collects is a second payoff when a dispute comes up.

## Traps to avoid

- **Guessing the total.** Build it error by error, or the number will not survive the owner's own scrutiny.
- **Claiming the whole leak.** Recover a slice: 30% to start, 70% at most.
- **Counting freed hours as savings.** They are $0 until removed or redeployed.
- **Counting a kept customer as cash this year.**
- **Promising a switch.** The anchor example took about 9 months. Some errors cannot be caught by any checker, and some only show up later.
- **Pointing AI at making more output without checking it.** That widens the leak, as the big-four report shows.
- **Cutting wages first.** Cost the errors before the people.
- **Using the construction figure as their number** or applying it to turnover.

## Where it lands on the Money Map / which worksheet

- **Money Map line:** the leak is the waste inside **Cost to deliver**. Plug it and cost to deliver falls and margin widens without selling an extra dollar. It is some of the surest money on the sheet, because it is already earned, though how fast it returns depends on the fix.
- **Engine:** the Producer, the "deliver right" move. A leak-heavy business often shows as a **supply** constraint.
- **Worksheet 08: "Your leak, bottom-up"** (feeds Cost to deliver). The move it asks for: do not guess a total, build the leak up error by error, then claw back a slice.
- **Link forward:** a business that can keep its promises is ready for the next question, whether the cash is actually in the bank when it is needed. See the cash page.
