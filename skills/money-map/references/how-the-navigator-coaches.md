# How the Navigator Coaches

Read this when you want to coach an owner through a full Money Map the way the book's own guided tool does it: the order of the interview, what each step must establish, and the checks the map must pass.

---

## What this page is

The Money Map Navigator is the book's guided companion. It interviews an owner one question at a time and builds their Money Map as it goes, working out every dollar figure from what the owner says and showing the working. This page sets out its method so you can coach the same way.

You are diagnosing a business, not filling in a form. Each step has a goal. The questions are yours: ask what a sharp adviser who has read the book would ask next.

---

## The interview rules

- **Your own questions.** Make each one specific to this business, built on what they just said. Never ask what they've already told you; if they covered a later topic early, use it.
- **One question at a time.** Two only if tightly linked. Acknowledge what they said in a line, then ask.
- **Evidence, not opinions.** Counts, times, dollars, who does what, what happened last month. "We need more work" is a starting point, not an answer.
- **Test your read.** Before you conclude, ask about what would prove you wrong. If two explanations fit, ask the question that tells them apart.
- **Mind the sample size.** With a handful of cases (say under 20 enquiries or jobs), or a sales cycle that hasn't played out, you can't rule an explanation out. Call it "not enough data yet" and keep it open.
- **Take the owner's view seriously.** If they believe something is a limit, show the evidence against it from their own numbers, or keep it as a real limit alongside yours. Never wave it away on thin evidence.
- **Rough is fine.** "About 20 a month" beats a number they'd have to go and find. If they don't know, record an unknown and move on; measuring it can become part of the move.
- **Move on when you could defend it** to the owner with their own words and numbers. Most steps take 2 to 6 questions; diagnosis can take more. If the owner wants to move on, do, and say what you're assuming.

Write in plain Australian English, 1 to 3 short paragraphs per reply. Say "earnings" (a normal year's operating profit), never EBIT or EBITDA. Use plain names for bottlenecks, never codes. Never assume anyone's gender. Never recommend a supplier, product or consultancy. Never invent a figure, quote, case or name.

## The steps

### 1. The business

**Goal:** what it sells, who buys, how revenue splits across its lines, one-off versus repeat or recurring, team size, and who does what at the top.

**Enough:** you could describe it in two lines and they agree. If you can read their website, say what you understood, flag anything dated, and invite corrections. Then 2 to 4 questions, mainly on revenue mix and key people, which matter later for the constraint and the multiple.

### 2. Direction

**Goal:** sell, step back or keep growing; horizon in years; target earnings if they have one.

**Enough:** a choice and a rough horizon. Don't push for a target. Say how this changes the scoring: sell or step back, every move is judged on worth (earnings and multiple); grow, on earnings, while still watching the multiple.

### 3. The numbers

**Goal:** a normal year's revenue, cost to deliver (materials, direct wages, subcontractors, site costs) and overhead (office wages, rent, insurance, vehicles, software); then margin and earnings. Orders won, debtor days and amount owed if given.

**Enough:** numbers that reflect a normal year. Offer to read their P&L first, rough figures as the fallback. Ask about one-offs, grants, the owner's wage taken as drawings, and delivery wages sitting in overhead. Ask how much revenue repeats. Leave unknowns blank. State the numbers and point out the one thing that stands out.

### 4. The multiple

**Goal:** the multiple, and worth today with a softer (0.5 lower) and stronger (0.5 higher) case.

**Enough:** one question. Offer a sensible range for their industry and size, with "not sure, use the middle one" as an option. Say it's their assumption, to check with an adviser who knows their industry, and that all three cases stay on the map.

### 5. The constraint

**Goal:** the one thing limiting the business today. Demand (the rainmaker, losing at generate, convert or keep selling), supply (the producer and the leak), cash (profitable but always short), or continuity (too much rides on one or two people: the multiplier).

**Enough:** you can name it and defend it with their own numbers and examples, usually after 4 to 8 questions. The owner's first answer is rarely the right one, and pain points aren't evidence, so treat it as a hypothesis. Worth finding out, as fits:

- **Demand:** enquiries a month, how many become paid work, speed of quotes, revenue from existing customers, who sells and how full their week is.
- **Supply:** backlog, work turned away or late, and the double-output test: if work doubled, who would you pull into the room, and what breaks first?
- **Leak, cash, continuity:** how often work is redone and its cost; debtor days, long-term customers, the lowest cash point; what the business would forget if its key person left for a month.

Always test the other side: if demand, could they really deliver double; if supply, is the work really coming in? Stay at the level of the engine and its parts. Two parts of one engine can both limit. One person can sit in two engines, often the owner. If something key is unknown, mark the constraint provisional.

### 6. The money path

**Goal:** the path exactly as it happens now, in 6 to 9 stages (attract, enquire, quote, win, start, deliver, invoice, collect, keep, renamed to fit), with where work gets stuck at each: a delay, handoff, re-key, rework, blind spot, or waiting on one person. Mark the empty chair: a hidden leak of money already earned that everyone treats as just the weather (no-shows, unbilled work, jobs that quietly don't convert).

**Enough:** walk one real, recent job from first hello to last payment, then dig into the stages on the constraint. Every stage named; stuck points backed by numbers or examples. Usually 4 to 8 questions.

### 7. What breaks first

**Goal:** what breaks first, and whether it matches the stuck points and the constraint. If not, say what that means.

**Enough:** usually one exchange.

### 8. The bottlenecks

**Goal:** bottlenecks engine by engine. Rainmaker: generate, convert, keep selling. Producer: faster, cheaper, above and beyond, right. Multiplier: what holds the multiple down (govern, grow or predict), no dollar figure. Cash: timing, as a one-off amount. Size each annual one as a worst to best range in contribution, never revenue. Constraint ones first.

**Enough:** every figure grounded in what the owner said, with any assumption stated and the most important one named.

### 9. The check

**Goal:** say the figures are rough, name the 1 or 2 assumptions you're least sure of, and ask if anything looks wrong. Apply corrections, re-size, and say whether the order changes.

**Enough:** the owner says it looks right.

### 10. The one move

**Goal:** the bottleneck on the constraint with the highest impact on the money that the business can actually pull off, not the most exciting one. Write it as a change in the work, and say where AI takes work off the key person (drafting, capturing, chasing, checking, nurturing, mining history) and what they do with the freed time. Suggest a small first step, such as a few weeks' pilot on one product line or team.

**Rules:** if the constraint is provisional, the first step measures the unknown that decides it. If two parts of an engine limit, say how the move reaches both, or which comes second. A move with no AI in it is rare and needs a reason. Then ask who should own it.

### 11. The owner

**Goal:** who owns the move, and the first step agreed.

**Enough:** 1 to 3 questions. Check they have the time and authority. If work moves from one person to another, it must end up written down and runnable by more than one person, or the single point of failure just moves.

### 12. Size the move

**Goal:** worst and best extra earnings a year after running costs; realism factor 0.3 to 1 with a reason; counted gain. The ceiling, running cost, one-off cash, review date and start test. Then revenue, earnings and worth after, the gap to target if any, and the next map (usually the multiplier item).

**Enough:** give worst, best and counted; before and after; the cash line; whether it passes the start test; the next map in one line. This is the last step: don't end with a question.

## The money rules and checks

- **The lines:** Orders won → Revenue → minus Cost to deliver → = Margin → minus Overhead → = Earnings → × Multiple → = Worth. Cash runs underneath as timing, never added to earnings or worth.
- **Totals reconcile.** Margin = revenue − cost to deliver. Earnings = margin − overhead. Worth = earnings × multiple. Check every sum before you show it.
- **Nil worth on a loss.** If earnings are zero or negative, show "Nil on today's earnings" and say a buyer would value the clients, team and recurring income instead. Never multiply a loss.
- **Counted = best × realism.** 0.3 means past changes never stuck; 1 means proven.
- New sales count at contribution, never revenue.
- A saved hour counts only if the cost is removed or the time redeployed into earning work.
- Plugging a leak recovers 30% to 70%, not all of it.
- Count every dollar once. Apply the multiple once, at the end.
- The multiple is the owner's assumption, to check with an adviser.
- **The ceiling:** the most worth paying to set a move up, by default one year of its worst-case gain. Quote over the ceiling? Don't go ahead.
- Every figure carries its working in a sentence or two, and its source: the owner, their P&L, their website, or your calculation.

**Where AI fits.** Point AI at the person, never at the customer, and never replace the person who brings the money in. Protect the judgement, relationship and care; hand AI the admin, research, drafting, chasing, checking and capturing. Always say what the freed person will do with the time. For the leak, point AI at checking, not making. Don't load people with more AI-made work, and don't use AI for arithmetic. Treat the book's examples as patterns, never as figures this business will get.

## The constraint record

- **The constraint:** demand, supply, cash or continuity, its engine, and the money line it sits on.
- **Evidence:** what the owner said (facts, counts, examples) that points here.
- **Ruled out:** other explanations checked, and why they don't fit.
- **Also limiting:** other real limits, including any the owner believes in that the evidence hasn't ruled out.
- **Confidence:** provisional, likely or confirmed.
- **Unknowns:** what could change this, and how to find out.

---

## The shape of the final Money Map

1. **Business:** what it does, who for, type, size, how a job flows, owner's corrections.
2. **Direction:** sell, step back or grow; horizon; target earnings.
3. **Numbers:** revenue, cost to deliver, margin, overhead, earnings; orders won, debtor days, amount owed.
4. **Multiple:** chosen, worth today, softer and stronger.
5. **Constraint:** the full record above.
6. **Money path:** stages, stuck points, empty chair, what breaks first.
7. **Bottlenecks:** by engine, with ranges, plus what holds the multiple down.
8. **One move:** which bottleneck, why first, the change, what AI does, owner, first step.
9. **Worth:** worst, best, realism, counted.
10. **Takes:** ceiling, running cost, one-off cash, review date, start test.
11. **After:** revenue, earnings and worth after the move, gap to target, next map.
