# Cash

Read this when the owner is profitable but short of cash, growing fast, carrying stock, waiting long to be paid, selling to much bigger customers, or about to spend (including on AI) without knowing when it pays back.

---

## Core ideas

- **Profit is not cash.** Profit is what the year adds up to on paper. Cash is what is in the bank the morning wages are due. The profit is real, just tied up where the owner cannot reach it.
- **Cash is the layer underneath the Money Map.** It is not a line like earnings or the multiple. It is the timing between money earned and money the owner can actually spend. It is never added to earnings or worth.
- **Cash pays for everything above it.** Growth, the next hire and the AI itself all cost cash before they make any, so a business can look healthy on every line and still run dry.
- **Growth eats cash.** Sell more and you buy more, carry more and wait longer to be paid. It runs driest when growing fastest.
- **Cash hides in three places,** none of which shows on the P&L:
  1. **In the work you have paid to deliver.** Stock in a product business: the cash left to buy it long before the P&L counts it as a cost. In project businesses (construction, trades, agencies) it is the subbies, wages and materials paid out before the customer pays. The project version is the more common one.
  2. **In what your customers owe you.** Traders, wholesalers and importers are often paid only when goods arrive, or later, with proof of delivery to chase. Every day a document is missing, the invoice cannot go out.
  3. **In the squeeze.** The biggest customers often pay slowest, 60 or 90 days, billed monthly, so missing a submission date by a day means being paid a whole cycle later. Signature line: "If your customer is 10 times your size, you are their bank."
- **AI does three jobs on cash: see it, speed it, protect it.**

## Questions to ask the owner

One at a time. Counts, days and dollars.

1. How many days of stock do you usually hold? (Or, for project work: how long between paying for a job and being paid for it?)
2. How many days, on average, do customers take to pay you? Who are the slowest, and how big are they compared with you?
3. How many days do you take to pay suppliers?
4. What must happen before an invoice can go out (proof of delivery, sign-off, a milestone)? How often does it slip?
5. How do you forecast cash now, who keeps it, and do decisions run through it first?
6. What is the lowest your bank balance is likely to get in the next 12 months, and when?
7. If you grew revenue next year, where would the cash to fund it come from?
8. How are supplier invoices checked before paying, and what happens when a supplier's bank details change?
9. What are you about to spend on, including AI, and what comes back, when?

## How to size it

Use Worksheet 09, "Your cash timing". Its fields:

- Days of stock held: ____ · days to get paid: ____ · days you take to pay: ____
- If you grew revenue by $____, the extra working capital it ties up is about $____
- Cash you could free by collecting faster or holding less: $____
- Net still to fund: $____
- **Your lowest cash point in the next 12 months: $____.** The book calls this the number that actually sinks businesses. Make sure the owner leaves with it.

**Collection speed sum:** annual revenue ÷ 365 × days shaved off how long customers take to pay. The book's illustrative sums: 7 days on a $3 million business frees about $58,000; on a $10 million business, about $192,000. Present it as a sum the owner runs on their own numbers, not a promised result.

**Growth sum (illustrative, from the book):** a business growing $6 million in sales can tie up something like $600,000 in extra stock and unpaid invoices before a new customer pays. Collecting faster and holding only the stock needed could free about $300,000 of that. Use it to show the shape, then work out the owner's own figure from their days.

**Counting rules:**
- Freed cash is **one-off**. It is the owner's own money in hand sooner, not new profit.
- **Never add it to earnings or worth,** or put it through the multiple. Show it on its own line.
- Prevented fraud or duplicate payments are cash kept, not earnings gained. Do not invent a likelihood or amount.

## Examples from the book

These are the book's examples. Never present their outcomes as what this owner will get.

- **The manufacturer's stock pile.** Raw material, some sitting 6 months, ordered on nerves; a huge slice of their cash frozen in the warehouse. They were about to spend $200,000 on forecasting software. Instead their own spreadsheets and exports went to a general AI assistant, worked by their CFO, procurement lead and one of the author's engineers. It found real usage rates, real supplier lead times and what to hold. They stopped over-ordering and the cash came back steadily over the following months. Their own data beat the expensive system.
- **The trader's paperwork.** A system tracks every shipment, chases proof of delivery, escalates what stalls, checks documents match and flags delays, so the invoice goes out the day it can. It needs a person watching and is not perfect, just relentless.
- **The author's firm's milestones.** On fixed-price enterprise work, a missed submission date pushes payment about 2 months. The author automated milestones early: tracking each, checking the work meets the promise, nudging project managers, and submitting sign-off before cut-off. Builders know it as the progress claim.
- **The living cash flow.** The author estimates about 80% of businesses he works with forecast cash on a hand-kept spreadsheet. His firm's director of finance and risk made every decision run through it, which clashed with an owner who moved fast. The fix: keep the sheet, give it a brain. AI runs hundreds of scenarios and watches the CRM, email and meetings; when an unbudgeted sponsorship came up, it modelled cost, payback and the new cash shape unasked.
- **The importer and the strait.** When conflict flared in the Middle East, the system modelled what a Strait of Hormuz closure or delay would do to an importer's shipments, landed cost and cash timing, ruling scenarios in or out as news moved. Signature line: "It does not forecast the war. It forecasts what the war does to your cash." No tool can say whether a strait will close; distrust one that claims to.
- **Collections that fit the customer.** Dumb reminders get ignored. Follow-up by email, SMS or call to suit each customer, with AI capturing the commitment ("paying Friday") and feeding it to the forecast.
- **Invoice checking.** Supplier invoices checked against order and contract, so nothing is paid wrongly or twice; the owner's own invoices checked before they go out, because a queried invoice is paid late.
- **Changed bank details.** An accountant in the author's community routinely ran his ledger through AI to flag anything unusual. It caught a supplier invoice with quietly changed bank details; the first payment was small, and two larger invoices to the same account were waiting behind it.

## Where AI does the work

Describe the job, never a product.

- **See:** a living cash forecast built on the owner's existing sheet and data, running many scenarios at once, watching inside the business (pipeline, conversations, unbudgeted decisions) and outside it (supply routes, currency, regulation) for anything that moves the cash.
- **Speed:** chasing the documents and sign-offs an invoice waits on; milestone tracking before cut-offs; collections follow-up matched to each customer, with commitments fed back to the forecast.
- **Protect:** matching supplier invoices to orders and contracts, catching duplicates, paying on real terms and not a day early, and flagging changed bank details or unusual amounts before money leaves.

**"Not for everyone."** The book says plainly that collections work is not for every business. If customers pay on the spot or always on time, leave it. Help the owner look honestly at whether they have a gap before sizing a fix.

## Traps to avoid

- **Adding freed cash to earnings or worth.** It is timing, one-off, and separate.
- **Treating cash admin as the small prize.** Admin that frees an hour is worth what is done with it; admin that moves cash puts money in hand this month.
- **Buying expensive software before reading their own data.** The manufacturer's lesson.
- **Trusting a forecast that claims to predict events.** Model the effect on cash, not the event.
- **Promising the illustrative sums.** Rerun them on the owner's numbers.
- **Ignoring the lowest cash point** while celebrating growth.
- **Breaking the one rule** (below), especially on AI spend.

## The one rule

**If cash is going out, the owner should be able to say what they expect to come back, and when.** Not a guarantee: a number and a date. "Cost of staying in business" or "resilience" are fine answers; spending on hope is not. Apply it hardest to AI spend: hiring an AI engineer or buying 55 licences with no payback worked out is a cash flow hole, not an AI story. A living forecast makes the check quick.

## Where it lands on the Money Map / which worksheet

- **Money Map:** the **Cash strip** running underneath the whole sheet. Never added to earnings, never multiplied into worth. Record freed cash on the summary's "cash $__ (one-off, separate)" line.
- **Constraint:** the **cash** constraint, "profitable but always short of it". Also check cash whenever a growth move is chosen, since growth ties cash up first.
- **Worksheet 09: "Your cash timing"** (feeds the Cash strip). The move it asks for: profit is not cash, so work out whether growth will stretch the business before it pays back.
