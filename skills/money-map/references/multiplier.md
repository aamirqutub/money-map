# The Multiplier: building what the business is worth

Read this when the owner wants to sell or step back, when too much rides on one person (often the owner), or when you reach the Multiple box on their Money Map.

---

## Core ideas

- **The third engine builds worth, not earnings.** The other two engines grow earnings; this one lifts the number they are multiplied by. Worth = earnings × multiple, and the multiple is the lever almost nobody pulls on purpose.
- **The irreplaceable person holds the worth down.** The person holding the business together is usually the one holding its worth down. Often it is the owner, but it can be a manager, a partner, or the long-serving person who knows where everything is. A business that cannot run without one particular person is worth less to everyone except that person.
- **The multiple prices risk and upside.** Govern and predict are about how little can go wrong. Grow is about how much room is ahead. The weakest of the three is where the multiple leaks.
  1. **Easy to govern:** a buyer can see and trust it. The money, the work and the knowledge sit somewhere visible, so the business can be proven quickly. Slow and murky is what buyers mark down.
  2. **Easy to grow:** revenue you can repeat on purpose. Referrals are wonderful but not a plan; ranking on search today is rented ground. Repeatable means keeping customers through revenue that recurs, or winning new ones through an engine you control. Recurring revenue is the most powerful single thing for the multiple.
  3. **Easy to predict:** the business holds its shape when a key person walks, and understands its own cause and effect, so next year is not a guess.
- **The spine: capture tacit knowledge.** What lifts the multiple is value that stays when a person goes home, goes on leave or leaves. Tacit knowledge is the judgement your best people use but never wrote down. Until recently it could not be got out of a head; AI can now draw it out and hold it in the company's memory. Signature line: "what you cannot capture, you cannot count." A buyer pays for what they can see and take over.
- **Capture the reasoning, not just the files.**

## Questions to ask the owner

1. **The walked-out question:** "If the person who holds the most in their head, maybe you, walked out for a month, what would the business forget? What would break first?"
2. Who is that person? If not the owner, what happens if they resign?
3. How many big decisions, clients and pricing calls end up on the owner's desk?
4. If a buyer sent a due diligence list tomorrow, how long would it take to answer, and who would stop work to do it?
5. How much revenue is one-off versus repeat or recurring? Is there a genuine ongoing need around what they sell (upkeep, servicing, inspection, advice) they have never charged for?
6. Where does new work come from: referrals, search, or an engine they can switch on? What share comes from the top 3 customers?
7. Among people doing the same job, how many are genuinely good, and what do the best do differently?
8. When a month jumps or drops, do they know why?
9. Is anyone retiring, going on leave or likely to leave in the next 1 to 3 years?
10. Is anything already recorded (calls, meetings, order history)?

## How to size it

**The scorecard (Worksheet 10).** Score each hook **weak**, **building** or **strong** from the owner's answers:

| Hook | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Govern | Knowledge in heads; diligence would take weeks; owner is the hub | Documented, transferable, provable fast, not dependent on one person |
| Grow | Lumpy, referral-led, a few customers dominate | Road ahead, repeatable revenue, customers not over-concentrated |
| Predict | Revenue lurches; a departure would hurt; nobody knows why months move | Repeat or recurring revenue; next year not a guess |

The weakest is where the multiple leaks and where the one move goes.

**Showing the multiple honestly.**
- Use normal-year earnings. Never multiply a loss.
- The multiple is **always their assumption**. Show worth three ways: market unchanged, a softer market, a stronger case.
- Show before and after at the same earnings so the multiple's effect stands alone. Where a move also lifts earnings, count each dollar once.
- Valuation is its own craft with more than one method, and the multiple moves with industry, buyer and timing. It is a direction, not a quote: they should check it with an adviser who knows their industry.

**The book's illustration (illustrative, never this business's number).** A manufacturer with earnings of $5.7 million is worth about $22.8 million at 4 times. Made easy to govern, grow and predict, the multiple can move to 5 times, about $28.5 million. Same earnings, $5.7 million more worth. The book calls 4 and 5 round numbers that show direction.

## Examples from the book

Retell in your own words; never present these numbers as what this business will get.

- **Due diligence in a day.** The author's business answered a full due diligence list the same business day, because the business lived in its memory, not his head. The other side said it usually takes 2 to 3 weeks, sometimes months. No new dollar came in; the business became easier to trust, buy and lend to. (Govern.)
- **Lumpy service into care plans.** An installer of engineering systems (machinery, robotic arms) had after-sale revenue that arrived like weather, and owners wanting to sell in 3 to 5 years. AI went through over 3 years of messy history (spreadsheets, unlogged work, old order emails) to find each client's pattern of use and failure; account managers gathered extra inputs for the top 5 clients; AI built worst, best and expected cases. An annual care plan was priced against the worst case with margin on the likely case, plus quarterly health checks and early warnings. Across 20 clients the plans would bring in roughly 40% more than those accounts averaged over 3 years, guaranteed, with the same service team. No sale has happened: it is a business becoming more sellable, not an exit. (Grow.)
- **8 salespeople, 3 good.** A consulting firm's top 3 could shape solutions the other 5 could not, and training never transferred it. AI read years of recorded calls, worked out what the top 3 did when a client hesitated and why deals turned, and handed it back. The other 5 started selling more like the best 3: higher earnings and less reliance on 3 people. (Grow.)
- **The retiring manager.** A manager with 30 years of judgement was retiring; his shadow was catching maybe a tenth of it. With his blessing, interviews, screen shares and watching him work went somewhere the business could ask a question and get his answer after he left. (Predict.)
- **The maternity handover.** At the author's firm, a system sat alongside a project manager's work for about 3 months before her leave, with her consent. When it saw a call it could not explain, it asked her why. Her successor inherited her reasoning. Signature line: "A handover gives the next person your files. This gives them your reasoning." (Predict.)
- **The five-why.** The author learned at Honda to ask why five times to reach the root cause. Busy businesses never find the time; AI can do it continuously and learn which lever moves which result. (Predict.)
- **Climb in, climb out.** The author is the bottleneck again on day one of every venture, so he climbs in, then works to climb out. Whenever he does a task himself he records it end to end (narrated screen recording, or audio in the field) and AI turns it into the guide, capturing dead ends as well as steps. His rule for his team: unless it is confidential, record conversations with him so his judgement spreads. Signature line: "Keep your secrets to yourself and you are not building a company. You are building yourself a job." A habit worth suggesting to the owner.

## Where AI does the work

- Drawing knowledge out of people (consented interviews, narrated screen recordings, audio of fieldwork, watching the work) and turning it into guides held in the company's memory.
- Sitting alongside someone's work, with consent, and asking why when it sees a call it cannot explain.
- Holding the money, the work and the knowledge in one visible place so diligence questions get fast answers.
- Mining order history for each customer's real pattern, building worst, best and expected ranges, and pricing a recurring plan safely.
- Running the recurring layer cheaply (monitoring, servicing, admin, routing the team), the cost that used to eat its margin.
- Reading recorded calls to codify what top performers do, then teaching it to the rest.
- Continuous root-cause analysis on why results moved.

## Traps to avoid

- **Moving the single point of failure.** The most common mistake: get the owner out of the seat, hand the whole dependency to one other person, call it solved. A buyer prices that exactly the same. The aim is a business that leans on no one.
- **A folder nobody opens.** Captured knowledge must live where the business can ask it a question.
- **Forcing a subscription.** The test is a genuine ongoing need, not "can I invent a fee". The recurring layer usually sits around the product, not in it.
- **Treating referrals or search ranking as a growth plan.**
- **Promising a multiple or implying an exit.** Show three ways, label it their assumption, send them to an adviser.
- **Capturing without consent.**
