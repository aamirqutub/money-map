# Why most AI spend vanishes

Read this when an owner says they've spent on AI and can't see the return, when they report "hours saved" as the win, when they want to start with admin automation, or when you need to explain why cost-cutting is the small prize.

---

## Core ideas

**The one question.** The book has one rule: if AI can't show up as a dollar on the P&L, in the cash, or in what the business is worth, it doesn't count. The author learned this the hard way. He rolled AI out across his own software company (12 years old, 55 licences, plenty of his own money). The team got faster and happier, and a year later the business sat flat. An adviser looked at the spend, asked why he couldn't see it, and said the six words the book is named after: "Forget AI. Show me the money."

**The winners did one thing first.** Across more than 500 owners over four years, the author saw the same solution make real money in one business and nothing in a near-identical one. It was rarely the technology. The owners who won had worked out, before spending a dollar, how many dollars it would make them. A number, not a hunch.

**Three reasons the money vanishes.**
1. **Wrong target.** It was aimed at a task, not a dollar.
2. **Wrong ruler.** It was measured in hours saved, not dollars earned.
3. **No map.** Nobody had shown the owner where the money in their own business actually lives.

Under all three sits the owner's own view: they see the business the way it has always worked, so they take its limits as fixed and look straight past the opening.

**The wrong target comes in two forms.** The book uses a dart that always hits whatever you aim at, so the mistake is always the aiming.
- *Shooting in the dark:* something clever that works but was never pointed at money (an HR policy assistant, a market-intelligence feed). The test is to ask "and then what does it make?" before building. The same capability can be aimed at marketing that pulls in enquiries, at keeping and growing existing clients, or at winning the right work. The one that makes money is the one where the outcome was decided before the build.
- *Aiming too low:* the admin, the data entry, the chasing. The book calls this pile ROBOTS (Repeated, Obligatory, Boring, Operational Tasks). It's a safe place to start and the book never says don't. Just know what it's worth.

"No AI use case is good or bad on its own." What counts is whether this one, in this business, moves money.

**Cost-cutting has a floor and a ceiling.** A saving only counts if the freed cost actually leaves: a role not backfilled, a contractor stopped, overtime ended. It's worth what's left after the AI's own running cost, which is never zero (it has to be run, watched and tuned, and the models change). And you can only cut a cost once, never below zero. Cut past the fat into the muscle and the business is worth less.

**Cash out once, or keep it growing.** A cost cut lifts profit and worth once, then it's done. The third target in the dart game is the cash pile that keeps growing after the dart lands: freed capacity pointed at winning and delivering more profitable work. The book's comparison: cut 20 of 150 people to save their wages, while a competitor keeps all 150, arms them with the same tools and points the freed capacity at profitable work. If that extra profit beats the 20 wages, they grow while you shrink. Compare profit against profit, over the same year.

**The three fates of a saved hour.**
1. *It evaporates.* Work expands to fill the day. Nothing is banked. This happens on its own.
2. *It becomes a cost cut,* but only if the cost leaves the building. Freed time is not a saved cost until the cost is gone.
3. *It turns into money,* when the freed time is pointed at work that earns.

"A freed hour is not a return. It is a chance to make one."

**The right ruler.** Count what the freed person produced in dollars, not the hours saved. **Don't judge by job title; size the prize by what their work controls.** Free someone whose time only costs money, and the ceiling is that cost. Free someone whose work makes, wins or keeps money, and the ceiling is whatever more of that is worth.

## Questions to ask the owner

- What have you pointed AI at so far? For each one, what number on your accounts did it move?
- If the honest answer is "none", where did the link to money break: the target, the delivery, or the time it still needs?
- Before you built it, did you decide what it should make? "And then what?"
- Where did the freed hours go? Can you trace them to anything?
- Did the cost actually leave, or is the person still there doing other things?
- Whose time did it free? What does that person's work control: cost, or sales, delivery, customers?
- If you're thinking of cutting roles, what could the same people make if you kept them and pointed them at earning work?

If the owner reports hours saved with pride, acknowledge it, then ask where those hours went.

## How to size it

**A cost saving: measure against earnings, not revenue.** The book's worked example (illustrative), a $30 million manufacturer it uses throughout:

| Line | Figure |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $30,000,000 |
| Margin | 12% |
| Earnings | $3,600,000 |
| Two people re-keying orders and invoices | $140,000 |
| AI takes about 80% of that work off their desks | |
| Freed cost actually removed | about $110,000 a year, before the AI's running cost |
| Saving against earnings | about 3% |

For the owner's version, use their numbers, count the saving only if the cost leaves, and take off the AI's running cost.

**The ceiling.** The book's second example: a suburban accounting firm carrying $500,000 a year in admin. Automate half and remove the freed cost, and $250,000 is gone, every year it stays out. But it can only be taken once. Even stripping $1.5 million across a whole business lifts profit and worth once, then stops.

**A freed key person.** Size it by the dollars their extra capacity produces, never by the hours or the wage saved. Count new sales at margin, not revenue.

## Examples from the book

- **The Valentine's agent.** The author's family agent haggled a plumber from $250 to $150 and booked him. Brilliant, and the trap in miniature: aimed at every pain point, not at the money.
- **The HR assistant.** The author's first internal build. Loved by the team, no effect on sales or output he could find in the P&L. A clever dart in the dark.
- **The audio reports and the "capture everything" engineering firm.** Both worked and moved nothing: nobody asked what the information should make.
- **Invoice matching, maker vs trader.** The same tool. A small maker with 7 or 8 supplier invoices a week saved a couple of hours: a coupon. A trader turning over millions, with about 60 a week and cash sitting in the gap between supplier bills and customer payments, got a cash-flow tool, where one missed mismatch could cost more than the build.
- **James the recruiter (anonymised).** Three months after a Copilot rollout his recruiters were saving close to a day a week each on notes. Asked where the time went, he couldn't say. Fate 1.
- **Robyn the car-accessories veteran (anonymised).** 22 years answering product enquiries. A system drafting replies from product knowledge, live pricing and stock, and past answers gave her about 30 hours a week back. Replies got fast and enquiry-to-sale conversion rose about 23% relative. She moved to big, tricky and key-account work and going after business. On the org chart she was "the admin"; her work actually controlled sales. (The book's picture of the lift, 1 in 5 becoming nearer 1 in 4, is a worked example, not the client's real starting rate.)

Never present these results as what the owner's business will get.

## Traps to avoid

- Accepting "hours saved" or "the team loves it" as a return, or counting a saving while the person is still on the payroll.
- Measuring a saving against revenue instead of earnings, or forgetting the AI's running cost.
- Treating a cost cut as repeatable. It's taken once.
- Sizing a person's value by their pay grade or title.
- Telling the owner admin automation is wrong. It's a fine start; it's just the small prize.
- Talking tools or models. Never recommend a product.
- Inventing a figure, or quoting the book's examples as a forecast.

## Where it lands on the Money Map

This is the diagnosis before the map. It explains why past spend didn't show up, and it sets the rules for sizing every later move: earnings not revenue, cost cuts only if the cost leaves, freed time counted in dollars produced. A cost cut lands in **overhead or cost to deliver**, once. A converted hour lands in **orders won or revenue realised**, and through earnings into **worth**. Invoice matching for a trader lands in **cash**.
