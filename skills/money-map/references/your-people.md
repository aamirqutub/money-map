# Your people

Read this when the owner asks who they can let go, when a move depends on staff actually using AI, when you need to discount a forecast for adoption, or when the plan points AI at a person or a customer conversation rather than at the work.

---

## Core ideas

**Multiply, do not cut.** Every time a business gets serious about AI, the same question turns up: who can we let go? The book calls it the smallest move on the table. Cutting a person saves their wage, and that saving repeats, so it is real, but it is capped: you can only cut so far before the business stops working. Making the same person count for far more has no ceiling, and almost nobody reaches for it. Both can be worth doing; the book's focus is the second. The coach's shorthand for this is **free, not fire**: free people from the grind so they can do more of the work that earns.

**The ratio to watch.** Take the business's profit and divide it by total wages. The book calls it one of the truest reads an owner has on the business, and it is the number to lift. Use the same profit figure each time (real operating profit) so you compare like with like. There are two ways to move it:

- **The lazy way:** shrink the bottom (fewer people, lower pay).
- **The powerful way:** grow the top (the same people producing far more).

The book's analogy: you do not make a country richer by having fewer citizens; you make it richer when each one produces more.

**Point AI at the work, never at the person.** Hand the machine the admin, research, chasing, forms and paperwork. Protect the judgement, the relationship and the care, and point the person at more of what only they can do. Never automate the human conversation that brings the money in. Get it backwards and, in the book's words, you burn money and insult your people in the same move.

**Adoption is the switch behind every number.** Every gain in this book rests on one fragile assumption: that people actually pick the tools up and use them. Most will not, at least not at first. The book calls this the most expensive problem in the whole method, because it turns every other number on or off. Licences, training and a perfect build are worthless if the tool sits untouched.

**What drives adoption:**
- **Quiet fear.** People rarely raise it in a training session: it will take my job; I am better at my craft than at writing prompts. It surfaces in one-on-one conversations, and it can take several rounds.
- **Everyone on their own clock.** Precise, control-minded people often struggle with something as probabilistic as AI. Pushing harder entrenches them. Some of the best come to it last and end up teaching everyone else.
- **Forcing backfires.** A tool a person resents is a tool a person quietly works around.
- **Fear from the top.** A careless joke from leadership about jobs can stall a project. The author's rule since: meet leadership first, coach them to talk honestly with their team before any tool goes near anyone, and if the whole team is in the room, put the fear on the table in that first conversation.

**Slop is a cost, not a saving.** Give people AI without teaching them to use it well and you get more of the wrong work: generic emails, bloated documents, first drafts dressed as finished, all needing someone to clean up. The book's line: the difference between a person "worth 5 times more and one who is just 5 times noisier".

**The honest bit.** Sometimes a role genuinely shrinks, and the book does not pretend otherwise. But that is the rare case, not the opening move.

## Questions to ask the owner

- Roughly what is your annual operating profit, and your total wages bill? (Then work out profit ÷ wages for them, and show the sum.)
- Who has to use this change day to day?
- Have you had an honest conversation with them about what it means for their job?
- Does anyone read it as a threat to their job? How would you know?
- The last time you rolled out a tool, did people actually use it? Honestly, how many?
- If we took the grind off this person, what would they do with the hours? What is that work worth?
- Is the AI being pointed at the customer conversation, or at the work behind it?
- Has anyone in leadership joked or hinted about jobs going?
- How will you know if the output is better, rather than just more?

## How to size it

**The ratio.** Profit ÷ total wages, using a normal year's operating profit. Track it over time. A move that grows the top is the one to chase.

**The adoption check (Worksheet 14).** Four plain checks:
1. Who has to use this?
2. Has the honest conversation happened? Y / N
3. Does anyone read it as a threat to their job? Y / N
4. Did the last rollout actually get used? Y / N

**The realism factor.** Discount the forecast to what is real: **counted gain = forecast (best case) × realism factor**, where the factor runs from **0.3** (the business has never done anything like this) to **1.0** (proven). Lower it if the people who have to use it have not been brought along. If the adoption boxes are unticked, the book is blunt: the most valuable move is the conversation, not the build.

**Price freed hours by where they go.** A freed hour is not a return. Value it at the worth of the work the person moves to, not the drudgery removed. If the cost is actually removed, count that instead; otherwise, by default, the hour evaporates.

## Examples from the book

**The toy distributor (Chapter 14, real, anonymised).** The business had spent about $20,000 on an AI that made cold calls, and it went nowhere. Asked to track salespeople's time for a week, they found about 2 to 3 hours a day with customers and 4 to 5 hours at the desk on research, proposals and chasing the internal team. They had automated the one thing you must never automate. The flip: a system that listened to calls, drafted the straightforward proposals and routed complex ones to the technical team. 3 months on, customer time had doubled from about 3 hours to 6, outbound sales were up 20%, about $150,000 more across 5 salespeople in a quarter (the client's reported result). Nobody was cut.

**The employability firm (Chapter 14, real, anonymised).** A delegate at a conference for employability professionals asked how AI could help him find more people, because he had a shortage. The real problem was keeping people in a gruelling job. Each caseworker got an assistant for notes, research, finding roles and paperwork, leaving them the human part. Team satisfaction went from 58% to 74% in about 3 months (measured). There is no retention figure yet. The book's illustrative cost of losing and replacing one person is $20,000 to $30,000.

**The travel agency (Chapter 6, real; anti-example).** An owner replaced his phone salespeople with AI. The cost came down, and so did the sales. Customers ring because they want a trusted human. The right move was the same AI pointed at the salesperson: the customer's history and a tailored offer on screen, and the follow-up drafted the moment the call ended.

**The author's own developers (Chapter 14).** Training and licences alone did not work. One-on-one conversations surfaced fear. It took 3 rounds to bring the most advanced people round, and 2 never came; their output stayed flat while the rest reached 4 to 5 times on the work AI suits. The author's rough figures: developers around 5 times, marketing at least 4, and more output is not the same as 5 times the profit.

**The finance expert and the forced tool (Chapter 14).** A finance expert said she was already efficient; months later she was building AI dashboards wired into the accounting system and teaching others. Separately, staff at a large company forced onto a tool said they hated it.

## Traps to avoid

- **Opening with headcount.** Cutting is real but capped. Lead with multiplying.
- **Automating the human conversation** that brings the money in (the cold caller, the travel agency).
- **Assuming adoption.** Bought, trained and built does not mean used. Discount the forecast.
- **Training instead of talking.** Fear does not come out in a group session.
- **Forcing it.** Resentment means work-arounds.
- **Leadership jokes about jobs.** Coach leaders first.
- **Counting output as profit.** 5 times the work is not 5 times the profit, and unguided use produces slop.
- **Pointing AI at a person as the problem.** Point it at the work.
