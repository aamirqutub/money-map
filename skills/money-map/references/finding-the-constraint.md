# Finding the constraint

Read this when the owner is telling you what is holding the business back, when they arrive with a list of pain points, or before you pick any move, so you diagnose the real constraint from evidence rather than from the first answer you hear.

---

## Core ideas

**Ask the fork question first.** Before anything else, the book asks every owner one question: are you looking to sell, or step back, in the next 2 or 3 years? The answer changes the whole conversation.

- **Yes, sell or step back.** Every move is judged by what it does to what the business is worth. Work both halves of that number, earnings and the multiple, and choose the levers that give the best combined result by the time the owner is at the table.
- **No, keep running it.** You are optimising the living business. The question becomes simpler and more urgent: where is the constraint?

**One constraint at a time.** Almost every business is held back by one of two things at a time:

- **Demand:** it cannot get enough work in the door. Start with the Rainmaker.
- **Supply:** it cannot get enough good work out the door. Start with the Producer (and the leak).

That one call tells you which end of the business to point everything at first, so the owner is not spreading effort across all three engines and moving none of them.

**Sometimes it is neither.** Two other constraints can bind instead:

- **Cash:** the business is profitable but always short of it. Start with the cash.
- **Continuity:** the business runs on one or two people who cannot easily be replaced, often the owner. Start by getting what those people know out of their heads and into the business. This is Multiplier territory.

**Don't start from a pain list.** This is the author's central flip in Chapter 13. Asking people where it hurts has a flaw you cannot see from the inside. People report what is top of mind, the thing that annoyed them last week. They cannot report the problem they stopped noticing years ago, the one they have built their day around, and they have no way of knowing whether something they have always lived with is even fixable. The result is a tidy list of the wrong size of problem. The book's signature line: "Map how the money moves, then find the empty chair."

So instead of ranking complaints, you map the whole business end to end and follow the money through it (see `money-path-and-the-one-move.md`).

**Diagnose with evidence, not opinion.** The book's method in the dentistry story was layered: read the system data first, compare like with like, form a hypothesis, then go and look with your shoes on, and talk to the people who actually do the work. Treat the owner's first answer as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

**Check with the people who do the work.** In the close (Chapter 16), the very first thing the owner does with a suspected constraint is take it to the people who do that work, "because they can see the empty chair you cannot". The owner sees the business from the top. The front desk, the estimator, the person chasing invoices see where the money actually stalls.

## Questions to ask the owner

Ask one at a time, and keep their answers in their own words.

**The fork**
- Are you looking to sell, or step back, in the next 2 or 3 years? Roughly when?

**Demand or supply**
- If work doubled next month, could you deliver it? Who would you pull into the room, and what would break first?
- If you had twice the capacity tomorrow, could you fill it?
- How many enquiries come in a month? How many become quotes? How many sign?
- How long from enquiry to quote? From quote to yes?
- How big is the backlog? How busy is the delivery team, honestly?
- How often is work redone, returned or corrected?

**Cash and continuity**
- Is the business profitable on paper but often short of cash? How many days to get paid?
- If the person who knows the most walked out for a month, what would the business forget? Is that person you?

**Evidence**
- Which systems hold the real record (bookings, jobs, billing, accounts)? What could you pull from them this week?
- Are there two teams, sites or people doing the same work with very different results? What is different about the better one?
- Who actually does the work at the point you suspect is stuck? What did they say when you asked them?

**Growth stress test**
- If the business keeps growing the way it has, what breaks first, and what would that cost you? A bottleneck that is only annoying at today's size can cap the whole business a year from now.

---

## How to size it

At this stage you are not sizing the move yet; you are sizing the evidence.

- **Ask for counts, not adjectives.** Enquiries a month, conversion at each step, days to quote, days to get paid, rework rate, backlog. Rough is fine ("about 20 a month").
- **Compare like with like.** The dentistry finding came from putting two near-identical centres side by side. Same patients, same system, same bookings, very different money per dentist. A gap between twins is a strong lead.
- **Say how sure you are.** Mark the diagnosis provisional, likely or confirmed, and name what you still don't know. With only a handful of cases, call it "not enough data yet" rather than ruling something out.
- **Hold two limits if the evidence says so.** If the owner is sure it is demand but the numbers point to supply, either show them the evidence from their own figures or keep their view as a real second limit.

## Examples from the book

**The dentistry group (Chapter 13, real, anonymised).** A keen dentistry group ran its own workshop before working with the author's firm and arrived with a long list of pain points, ready to rank them and get going. The author thanked them, meant it, and asked to walk the whole business end to end first.

- **System data.** The team put AI to work reading the practice management system and the billing. A few days in, the author's process engineer spotted that one centre was billing about 30% more per dentist, every day, than the others.
- **Ruling out the easy answer.** The author's first guess was a wealthier patient base. The booking data said otherwise: same kind of patients, same booking system, same number of bookings. On paper the centres were twins.
- **Shoes-on site visit.** The busy centre's calendars were fuller, with fewer holes, but the data could not say why. So they drove out and talked to the front desk.
- **The finding.** A receptionist who had brought the habit from a previous practice ran her own follow-up system: a message a week out, a reminder the day before, an early clear yes or no from anyone wavering, and a freed slot offered to another patient 2 days ahead. Nobody had put it on a pain list, because everywhere else the empty chair was "just the weather".
- **The fix.** Her habit was built into a system that messages and calls every patient, spots the ones at risk of not turning up, confirms them early, and offers freed slots to the next patient. Turn-up rates and billable hours per dentist rose across the group. No new patients, no new dentists. The book gives no dollar figure for the result; do not invent one.

The lesson to carry: working the team's list would have walked straight past the biggest prize in the group.

## Traps to avoid

- **Ranking the pain list.** It feels productive and respects the team's effort, but it surfaces the wrong size of problem. Thank them for it, then map the money.
- **Accepting the first answer.** "We need more leads" is a starting point. Owners who are certain they need more leads often have fine leads and poor conversion.
- **Explaining away the anomaly.** The author's own first reaction to the 30% gap was to shrug it off. Check the data before reaching for the comfortable explanation.
- **Staying at the desk.** Data shows where the gap is; it often cannot show why. Someone has to go and look and talk to the people doing the work.
- **Spreading across all three engines.** Pick the end of the business the constraint sits at and point everything there first.
- **Ignoring the exit horizon.** A move that lifts earnings but leaves the business dependent on the owner may do little for what it sells for.
- **Treating weather as fixed.** No-shows, cold quotes and unbilled work feel like how the industry works. Those are exactly where the empty chair hides.

## Where it lands on the Money Map

- The fork answer sets the **Heading** line (sell, step back or grow, and when) and decides whether you judge moves by **worth** or by **earnings**.
- The constraint fills **Holding you back**, with its evidence and how sure you are.
- Demand points you at the **Rainmaker** and **Orders won**; supply at the **Producer**, **Revenue realised** and **Cost to deliver** (the leak); cash at the **Cash** strip; continuity at the **Multiple**.
