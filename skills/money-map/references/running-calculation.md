# The running calculation

Read this when you are adding up what a move is worth, building the owner's before-and-after, or checking that a gain has not been double counted.

*From Chapter 12 of* Forget AI. Show Me The Money. *by Aamir Qutub. Coaching notes, not the book text.*

---

## Core ideas

**The whole book on one business.** Chapter 12 adds up everything the engine chapters did on the one manufacturer followed since Chapter 5, on a single page. It is deliberately short and visual: two moves, counted honestly.

**Two levers, in order.** First grow what the business earns (rainmaker and producer, plus plugging the leak). Then grow what a dollar of those earnings is worth (the multiplier lifts the multiple). Signature line: *"Earn more. Then make every dollar of it worth more."*

**Counted once, not stacked.** The earnings gain is the combined result of the earning engines working together, counted once. It is not each chapter's win added on top of the last. The author calls stacking the way consultants get to numbers nobody ever sees.

**Cash is separate.** Growth ties up working capital: more stock, more work in progress, more money owed before it lands. Cash funds the growth and decides whether the business survives it long enough to bank it. It is never a second gain added to earnings or worth.

**Full-year figures.** The gain is the full-year figure once the changes are running, not the first year while they are being set up.

**The multiple is applied once,** at the end, and it is an assumption. It moves with the buyer and the market.

**Sensitivity, not a prize.** The gap between worth today and worth after is a sensitivity check on the owner's own assumptions. It shows what moving both levers could be worth if those assumptions hold. It is not a promise and not something they are owed.

## Examples from the book: the $30 million manufacturer, end to end

All figures are the book's **worked example and illustrative**. Never present them as what the owner's business will get.

**Start.** Revenue $30 million. About 12 cents in the dollar is earnings, so $3.6 million a year. At around 4 times (an assumption carried through the book), worth is about $14.4 million.

**Earn more.** The rainmaker gets its time back and work it was too slow to quote starts closing, so revenue climbs from $30 million toward $36 million. The producer delivers more without adding a single person, and the leak (rework and waste) stops eating margin. The book's earnings bridge:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Extra revenue realised | $6,000,000 |
| Contribution on it, about 35% | +$2,100,000 |
| Less genuinely new capacity to deliver it (some overtime, a contractor, extra kit) | −$125,000 |
| Less the AI, all-in (the people running it, tokens, consultants) | −$275,000 |
| Plus the leak plugged (rework recovered) | +$400,000 |
| **Extra earnings a year, after the AI's own running cost** | **+$2,100,000** |
| Earnings | $3,600,000 → $5,700,000 |

Three points the book is careful about:

- **The capacity line is small on purpose.** The extra $6 million is delivered by people and plant already paid for, freed up by taking the grind off them, not by a new line or shift. That is why almost all the new contribution falls through.
- **The 35% is the margin on the extra sales alone.** The $400,000 of recovered rework is a separate line, not folded inside it.
- **No existing wage is counted again** in the $125,000.

At the same 4 times, worth goes from about $14.4 million to about $22.8 million.

**Cash to fund the growth** (kept in its own band):

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Working capital the growth ties up | about $600,000 |
| Released by collecting faster and holding less stock | about $300,000 |
| Net cash still to find | about $300,000 |
| One-off setup, staged, kept separate | about $250,000 |

The ongoing AI running cost sits in the earnings bridge, not here. Do not count this cash release again inside the earnings-based worth.

**Worth more.** Over the multiplier chapters the business learned to run without its owner, put what its people knew into institutional memory, and was wired to watch itself. To a buyer, bank or partner it stops being a risky thing living inside its owner. The multiple can move from 4 to 5 (an assumption, never a promise). Same $5.7 million earnings, worth about $28.5 million.

**The result.** About $2.1 million more earnings; about $14.1 million more worth, nearly double. Growing earnings did the larger share, around $8.4 million. Lifting the multiple added another $5.7 million, without a single extra dollar of earnings.

## Questions to ask the owner

- "What are your earnings and the multiple we settled on? That gives us worth today."
- "Which one move are we sizing? What does it change in the work?"
- "Is that gain a full-year figure once it's running?"
- "What extra revenue does it realise, and what margin do you make on extra sales alone?"
- "What genuinely new cost does delivering it add: overtime, contractors, kit? Leave out wages you already pay."
- "What will the AI cost to run each year, all-in: people, subscriptions, help?"
- "Is there a leak this move plugs? How often does the error happen, and what does each one cost to put right?"
- "Does the move make the business easier to govern, grow or predict? What would a buyer see differently?"
- "How much cash does the growth tie up, and how much could faster collection or less stock free?"
- "What one-off setup would it take?"
- "How much bigger do you want this to get, and by when?"

## How to size it: running it on the owner's numbers

1. **Now.** Orders won → revenue realised → minus cost to deliver → margin → minus overhead → earnings → × multiple (their assumption) → worth today.
2. **The earnings bridge for the one move.** Extra revenue realised × margin on extra sales, minus genuinely new delivery cost, minus the AI's full running cost, plus any leak recovered (a slice, not all of it: build it up error by error, then claw back 30% to start, 70% at most). Count new sales at margin, never revenue.
3. **Check for double counting.** If two moves touch the same work, count the combined effect once. A saved hour only counts if a cost actually leaves or the time is pointed at earning work.
4. **Earnings after = today + the bridge.** Full-year figure, once running.
5. **The multiple.** Keep it the same unless the move makes the business clearly easier to govern, grow or predict. Then show worth **three ways**: market unchanged, evidenced improvement, softer market.
6. **Worth after = earnings after × multiple.** Apply the multiple once.
7. **Cash band underneath:** growth ties up $__, faster collection frees $__ (revenue ÷ 365 × days saved), net still to fund $__, one-off setup $__. Never added to earnings or worth.
8. **Use ranges** (worst and best) and say plainly which assumptions drive the result. If earnings are zero or negative, worth on earnings is nil.
9. **Frame the gap** as a sensitivity check on their own assumptions, then turn to what it takes to earn it.

## Traps to avoid

- **Stacking each engine's win** on top of the others instead of one combined gain.
- **Counting revenue as earnings.** Use contribution margin on the extra sales.
- **Forgetting the AI's own running cost,** or the new capacity needed to deliver.
- **Folding the leak into the margin** and then counting it again.
- **Adding cash to worth,** or putting AI running costs in the cash band.
- **Using first-year figures** as if they were the steady state.
- **Applying the multiple twice,** or raising it without evidence the business got steadier.
- **Presenting the manufacturer's numbers as the owner's,** or the result as a promise or a prize.
- **Inventing a softer-market figure for the manufacturer.** The book shows only the 4 times and 5 times cases; build the softer case on the owner's own numbers.
